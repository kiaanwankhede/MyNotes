# RAG explained: why, and what the parts are
> lead: A model cannot know your documents. RAG looks the right pages up first and answers from them. This chapter shows the two stages, the ways basic RAG fails, and the eight parts you choose between.
meta: RAG | Meridian slides, module 4 | 15 min read

## Why it matters

Companies keep rules in PDFs. A general chatbot has never seen them, so it either refuses or guesses. RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) is the open-book exam: fetch the relevant documents first, then answer from them. The answer is only as good as what is retrieved.

:::key Prerequisites
Read **Python and Agent Foundations** chapter 2 first (embeddings, context window, hallucination). The examples come from Meridian, a fictional procurement app; its full tour is the last chapter of **Agentic AI with LangChain**.
:::

## Core ideas

### The two stages

the model cannot know your documents; RAG is look up, then answer. Two stages: index once (read, chunk, embed, store), then per question (embed the question, find the closest chunks, prompt, answer). Keyword search misses what meaning search finds (for "who has to approve a very expensive order" they share no useful word). A "meaning map" shows 22 sentences as dots. Eight things go wrong, and a confident wrong answer usually means the right chunk was not retrieved.

### Types of RAG, and when basic RAG hurts

basic RAG hurts when the words differ, the answer is split across chunks, or the right chunk is not first. Improve it **before** the search (change the question), **during** (change what is searched or how), **after** (clean the results) or **beyond** it. The site compares **thirteen variants** (I saw these among them: multi-query, contextual compression, HyDE, query decomposition, follow-up rewrite, hybrid search and reranking) and names four bigger ideas, of which I saw three: corrective RAG grades the retrieved chunks and tries again if they are poor, agentic RAG lets an agent decide whether, what and how often to search, and graph RAG works from a graph of the documents. Which first: right chunk found but not first, try reranking; users phrase things differently, try multi-query or HyDE; several-part questions, decomposition. Meridian uses three: similarity, multiquery and contextual (first-run times 7.1, 12.0 and 9.7 seconds).

### The eight parts, and choosing tools

a RAG system has **eight parts** (parse, chunk, embed, store, retrieve, rerank, generate, evaluate), each a decision. For embeddings check dimensions (768 here), languages, input length, hosted or open, and the rule that **one model must index and search**. Choosing a vector database: how many vectors, do you already run Postgres or Elasticsearch, filtering, managed or self-run, cost.

## The instructor's plain-language RAG guide

*From "RAG, Properly Explained", part 4 of the instructor's series (read in full). Its figures about Anthropic's research are as the guide states them.*

- **The problem.** Your company's refund policy, contracts and wiki were never in any model's training data, and retraining on them is slow, expensive and would absorb private text into the model. What you need is to hand the model the right document at the moment it is asked.
- **Closed book versus open book.** A plain LLM sits a closed-book exam from memory, so its answer can be stale or invented. RAG makes it open-book: the relevant pages are handed over first and it answers from them.
- **The library analogy, one to one.** Books are your documents; right-sized chunks are the sections; the librarian is the embedding model; the card catalog is the vector database; the research assistant is the generation model (Claude). The librarian appears twice: once cataloguing, once looking up your question.
- **Setting up (once).** Cut documents into chunks (too small loses meaning, too large blurs topics), embed each chunk, and store the vectors. Chunks about the same topic land near each other even if they share few words.
- **Using it (every question).** Embed the question with the **same** model, find the nearest chunks, assemble a prompt (system instruction to answer only from the context, the chunks, the question) and send it to the model. Switching embedding providers without re-embedding everything breaks retrieval silently.
- **The whole pipeline.** Raw data, information extraction (OCR, PDF parsing, crawlers), chunking, embedding, vector database; then query, embedding (same model), vector database, relevant chunks, model, response.
- **Do you even need RAG?** If the whole knowledge base fits in the context window (the guide, following Anthropic's guidance, says under roughly 200,000 tokens, about 500 pages), you can paste it in and use prompt caching. Use RAG when the knowledge base is larger, changes constantly, or must be scoped per user or permission. Many systems use both.
- **Vector databases.** Their job is storing vectors and answering "what is nearest" in milliseconds. The guide names Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, pgvector and Milvus, and says nearly all support hybrid search now, so the choice is mostly managed versus self-run and whether it fits a database you already run.

## From the Meridian slides in full: chapters 13 to 15

*Module 4 of the Meridian course slides (125 slides) was read in full on 31 Oct 2026. This section adds what the earlier digest left out.*

**Chapter 13, the idea, with real recorded answers.** Asked "what payment terms do servo motor suppliers get at Aldermoor?" with no documents, the model (gemini-3.8-flash, temperature 0, 10 Oct 2026) gave generic advice that such terms are private and negotiated. With the procurement policy page in the prompt, it answered exactly: Net-45 for low-risk vendors with a credit score of 75 or more. The slides then test the prompt's honest exit across combinations of pages. With the instruction "answer only from the context, otherwise say you don't know" switched on, the tariff page alone gave "I don't know", and the policy page alone gave Net-45 even with the tariff page as a distraction. With the instruction off, the model answered from the policy page when it had it, and without it gave generic advice. One sentence in the prompt is what makes an honest refusal reliable.

The two stages in the project's settings: index once (PyPDFLoader reads each page and remembers the file name; `RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter` cuts chunks of about 1,000 characters with 100 overlap; an embedding model returns 768 numbers per chunk; Chroma saves numbers and text, with stable ids so re-indexing replaces) and then per question (embed the question with the **same** model, take the nearest three chunks, build the prompt, let Gemini answer). Real closeness scores sit in a narrow band (0.85 for the right sentence against 0.55 and 0.57 for the others), so compare scores with each other, never with zero.

On 22 sentences from the four sample documents and eight questions, keyword search found a correct sentence in its top three for 2 questions and meaning search for 8 (the instructor's own judgement of "correct"). For "who has to approve a very expensive order?" the sentences share no useful word, yet meaning search finds the EUR 250,000 and EUR 1,000,000 approval lines. For "when do we pay servo motor suppliers?" the nearest by meaning were the right sentence (0.67), the servo-motor tariff line (0.67) and a wrong-risk-class line (0.66), which is why ranking matters.

**The eight things that go wrong, with the symptom, cause and fix.**

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confident but wrong | The right chunk was not retrieved, or a near miss came first | Look at the retrieved chunks before blaming the model; try another mode, a larger k, or a reranker |
| "I don't know" though the file has the answer | The chunk was never found: wrong chunk size, a scanned PDF with no text, very different words | Check the PDF has real text, try smaller chunks, try multi-query |
| Mixes two documents | Chunks from different topics came back together | Smaller k, rerank, filter by source, or contextual compression |
| Ignores the files | The prompt does not force the context | Keep "if you don't know based on the context, say you don't know" |
| Worse after changing the embedding model | Index and search use different models | Re-index everything with the new model; never mix |
| The same sentence twice | A file was indexed twice with random ids | Stable ids from file, page and position |
| Slow | Each model-based step (rewrite, trim, rerank) adds a call | Use plain similarity unless the question needs more |
| Key sentence cut in half | A chunk boundary fell mid-sentence | Overlap, recursive splitter, chunks large enough for a whole idea |

**Chapter 14, thirteen variants, each with a pipeline, a best use and a cost.** Basic RAG hurts three ways: different words ("expensive order" versus "purchases above EUR 250,000"), wrong order (the right chunk is third), and noise or gaps. The variants improve it in four places.

| Where | Variant | Idea | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before the search | Multi-query | The model writes 3 rewrites; search all 4; merge | 1 extra model call and 4 searches (12 s against 7 s in the run) |
| | HyDE | Search with an imagined answer (never shown or trusted) | 1 extra call |
| | Query decomposition | Split a many-part question; search each; merge | 1 call plus several searches |
| | Follow-up rewrite | "And for moderate risk?" becomes a standalone question | 1 call per turn |
| During | Hybrid | Keyword and meaning rankings fused (often reciprocal rank fusion) | A second index to keep |
| | MMR | Relevant but unlike each other | A little computation |
| | Parent pages | Search small pieces, return the whole page | Bigger prompts |
| After | Reranking | Fetch many, a stronger judge reorders (for example 20 down to 3) | Latency and a reranker call |
| | Contextual compression | Fetch 10, the model copies only the relevant sentences (one call per chunk); one 844-character chunk shrank to 193 | Many small calls |
| | Corrective RAG | Grade the results; if poor, rewrite and search again | Extra calls |
| Beyond | Agentic RAG | An agent decides whether, what and how often to search | Most expensive, least predictable |
| | Graph RAG | Entities and relations; follow links | Building and maintaining the graph |
| | Multimodal RAG | Index images and tables too | Heavier parsing |

Which to try first: right chunk found but not first, rerank; users phrase things differently, multi-query or HyDE; questions with several parts, decomposition; chat follow-ups, rewrite; exact names or codes missed, hybrid; long noisy chunks, compression; near-duplicate results, MMR; the answer needs surrounding text, parent pages; the model answers when the documents don't cover it, corrective RAG. Meridian implements three (similarity, multiquery, contextual), timed at 7.1, 12.0 and 9.7 seconds in one run and 5.0, 9.8 and 5.8 in another: the numbers varied, the order held.

**Chapter 15, the tools.** The eight parts (parse, chunk, embed, store, retrieve, rerank, generate, evaluate), with the slide's example tools:

| Part | Examples | In Meridian |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parse | pypdf, PyMuPDF, Docling, Unstructured, LlamaParse, Google Document AI | `PyPDFLoader` (pypdf) |
| Chunk | Recursive, semantic or layout-based splitters | Recursive, 1,000 and 100 |
| Embed | Gemini, OpenAI, Cohere, Voyage, BGE-M3, Qwen3-Embedding, Nomic | `gemini-embedding-001`, 768 numbers |
| Store | Chroma, FAISS, pgvector, Qdrant, Weaviate, Milvus, Pinecone, Elasticsearch or OpenSearch | Chroma locally, Vertex AI Vector Search in the cloud |
| Rerank | Cohere Rerank, BGE reranker, cross-encoders, a model as judge | Not in the project (shown in the lab) |
| Evaluate | RAGAS, DeepEval, TruLens, LangSmith, your own question list | A five-question lab |

Embedding models: check how many numbers, languages, how much text it accepts, hosted versus open, and use one model for both indexing and search; leaderboards such as MTEB change every few months, so test on your own documents. Vector databases store vectors with text and metadata, find neighbours, filter and update; exact search compares every vector, approximate search (often an HNSW index) looks at a clever subset, faster at millions with a small chance of missing the best match. Five questions to choose one: how many vectors (thousands: Chroma or FAISS; millions: Qdrant, Weaviate, pgvector or managed; billions: Milvus or managed); do you already run Postgres (pgvector) or Elasticsearch or OpenSearch; do you want to run servers; do you need filters or keyword search; where must the data live. Region, privacy and contracts can decide before performance does. Frameworks save plumbing, not thinking: LangChain here, LlamaIndex for indexing and querying, Haystack for typed pipelines, DSPy for tuning, or none.

## Interview corner

:::qa What is RAG?
Find the right pages first, then answer from them: an open-book exam. Chunks of your documents are embedded, stored in a vector database and retrieved by meaning, then given to the model with the question.
:::

:::qa How does it stop the model making things up?
The prompt says to answer from the retrieved context and to say "I do not know" otherwise.
:::
